Quartiles and Percentiles

Move beyond the center of a dataset and learn where observations lie, how widely they are distributed, and which values are unusual.

August 30, 2026•Ahamed Learning Notes

Mean, median, and mode measure the center of data. Quartiles and percentiles help us understand how the data are distributed around that center.

These ideas help us determine:

  • How spread out the data are.
  • Where most observations lie.
  • Whether values are unusual or potential outliers.
  • How features should be normalized or preprocessed.

1. Percentiles

What is a percentile?

A percentile tells us the value below which a certain percentage of observations falls.

The X-th percentile: approximately X% of the data is less than or equal to that value.

Example: exam scores

43, 52, 61, 69, 76, 84, 93

The 50th percentile

The 50th percentile is the value with half of the data below it. It is also the median.

P50 = 69

The 90th percentile

The 90th percentile is a value near the upper end of these scores. Interpreted generally, about 90% of observations are at or below it and about 10% are higher.

P90 = 87.6

Using linear interpolation, 87.6 lies 40% of the way from 84 to 93. In a larger student population, this would mean that only about 10% of students scored higher than the 90th-percentile score.

2. Quartiles

Quartiles divide sorted data into four equal sections. Each section contains approximately 25% of the data. 

|---------|---------|---------|---------|
    25%       50%       75%       100%
     Q1        Q2        Q3

Q1: first quartile

Q1 is the point with approximately 25% of the data below it and 75% above it. It is the 25th percentile.

Q1 = P25

Q2: second quartile

Q2 is the median, or the 50th percentile.

Q2 = P50

Q3: third quartile

Q3 is the point with approximately 75% of the data below it and 25% above it. It is the 75th percentile.

Q3 = P75

3. Calculating quartiles by hand

Consider this already sorted dataset:

6, 11, 17, 23, 28, 34, 39, 46, 52

There are nine observations.

Step 1: find the median (Q2)

6, 11, 17, 23, [28], 34, 39, 46, 52
Q2 = 28

Step 2: find Q1

Exclude the median and examine the lower half:

6, 11, 17, 23
Q1 = (11 + 17) / 2 = 14

Step 3: find Q3

Exclude the median and examine the upper half:

34, 39, 46, 52
Q3 = (39 + 46) / 2 = 42.5

4. Interquartile range (IQR)

The interquartile range measures the spread of the middle 50% of observations.

IQR = Q3 - Q1

For the hand-calculated example:

IQR = 42.5 - 14 = 28.5

Why is the IQR important?

Consider these incomes:

$30k, $35k, $40k, $45k, $50k, $10 million

The extreme income makes the mean very large. The IQR focuses on the middle 50% instead, making it much more robust against outliers.

5. The box-plot connection

A box plot summarizes a distribution using its quartiles:

       |------|========|------|
              Q1  Q2  Q3
  • The box extends from Q1 to Q3 and contains the middle 50%.
  • The line inside the box marks Q2, the median.
  • The whiskers show the typical range outside the box.
  • Points beyond the whiskers may be plotted as possible outliers.

6. Quartiles in machine learning

A. Detecting outliers

The common 1.5 × IQR rule flags a value as a potential outlier if:

x < Q1 - 1.5(IQR)

or

x > Q3 + 1.5(IQR)

Suppose Q1 = 20 and Q3 = 40.

IQR = 40 - 20 = 20
Lower boundary = 20 - 1.5(20) = -10
Upper boundary = 40 + 1.5(20) = 70

Values below -10 or above 70 are potential outliers. A flagged value deserves investigation; it is not automatically an error.

B. Feature analysis

Suppose you analyze these house prices:

$100k, $120k, $150k, $180k, $200k, $5 million

Quartiles help reveal:

  • Where most houses are priced.
  • Whether the $5 million house is unusual.
  • Whether the feature may need a transformation.

C. Robust scaling

Standardization uses the mean and standard deviation:

z = (x - μ) / σ

Both quantities can be affected strongly by outliers. Robust scaling instead uses the median and IQR:

x' = (x - Q2) / IQR

Because the median and IQR resist extreme values, robust scaling is often better suited to skewed features with outliers.

7. Python example

import numpy as np

data = np.array([6, 11, 17, 23, 28, 34, 39, 46, 52])

Q1 = np.percentile(data, 25)
Q2 = np.percentile(data, 50)
Q3 = np.percentile(data, 75)
IQR = Q3 - Q1

print(Q1, Q2, Q3)
print(IQR)

Output with NumPy's default percentile method:

17.0 28.0 39.0
22.0
Why does NumPy differ from the hand calculation? Quartiles do not have one universal calculation convention for a finite dataset. The hand method above excludes the median and takes the medians of the two halves, producing 14 and 42.5. NumPy's default percentile method uses positions and linear interpolation, producing 17 and 39. Always state or consistently use the chosen method when exact quartile values matter.

Key takeaways

  • A percentile locates a value within a distribution.
  • Q1, Q2, and Q3 are the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles.
  • The IQR measures the spread of the middle 50%.
  • The 1.5 × IQR rule identifies potential outliers.
  • Median-and-IQR scaling is resistant to extreme values.
  • Different quartile conventions can produce different answers.
